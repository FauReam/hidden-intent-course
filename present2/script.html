Where this picks up · relation to talk 1

Talk 1 set up two threads. Thread 1: a deceptive model knows the truth on the inside and does not say it — it "knows" mid-stack and "decides not to say" late. Thread 2: small decision models (no text generation, only judgment plus a score) reading the big model's hidden semantics. The midterm does not re-motivate; it delivers evidence: is that "truth direction" a real capability or just pretty on its home dataset? And it ends with the first catch on an actual deceptive model: the Taboo model really generates its evasive answer, and our monitor flags the secret in the same run.

Pacing: scenes 1–3 are setup (title, 30-second recap, the three probe formulas), scene 4 is the main course (the 11×11 transfer matrix — a full two minutes), scenes 5–7 are a three-step interrogation (failures cluster on negation → label-free CCS repairs it → the text baseline proves we are not reading surface words), scenes 8–9 land and stay honest (layer curve, limitations), scene 10 is the live catch, scene 11 closes with the final's two dishes. The paper on the right sits still the whole time — only its scroll position and the amber frame move: wherever the talk goes, the frame follows.

The one-liner

In LLaMA-2-13B, the truth direction travels across topics and languages (0.97 on the hardest transfer), it breaks exactly on negated statements (0.33), a label-free probe fixes it (0.97), and a text classifier proves the answer is not in the words (chance on 10/11). The knowledge is in the model, not on the page.

And the new second sentence: on the Taboo model, the model really generates a fluent, informed answer that never contains the secret word — and the logit-lens monitor still catches the secret in the middle layers. Says nothing, knows everything; caught anyway.

Primer · every term in one sentence

Written for a zero-background audience. The italic line is a speakable definition; the paragraph under it is your own explanation.
Probe
a tiny classifier reading hidden states
A very small classifier: input is the activation vector at one layer, output is a judgment (true/false). It is our "small decision model" — it generates no text, it only judges.
Generalization / transfer
train on one topic, test on another
Train on one topic, take the exam on another. If it transfers, the direction captures "true" itself — not the memorized quirks of one question type.
Held-out accuracy
same dataset, unseen examples
80% of a dataset for training, the unseen 20% for the exam. This is the floor check: if a probe fails in-distribution, transfer is out of the question.
Chance level
0.5 for a balanced binary task
With two balanced classes, guessing also scores 0.5. Accuracy near 0.5 = the probe reads nothing; below 0.5 = it is confidently reading the opposite way.
CCS · contrast-consistent search
a statement and its negation must come out opposite
The unsupervised probe: no labels at all, only the requirement that a statement and its negation get opposite judgments. Its safety value is the selling point — on the day we monitor hidden intent, there are no labels.
Negation
"The Eiffel Tower is in Rome" → "It is not the case that …"
Prefix the original sentence with "It is not the case that". The truth label flips while the surface words barely change — a natural test of "reading semantics vs reading words".
TF-IDF baseline
classify the raw string, never touching the model
The control group: never touch the model; turn the raw sentence into word-frequency vectors and feed logistic regression. Whatever it scores is exactly how much "the answer was written on the page" is worth.
Layer sweep
train the probe at every layer, compare
Train the same probe independently at twelve layers and see where it reads best. One-sentence conclusion: the middle layers know most — which is also the answer to "at which layer does the deceptive model lie".
Taboo model
a Gemma model + a small public adapter: knows a secret word, uses it, never says it
Cywiński et al. 2025 released LoRA adapters (gold / moon / cloud) on gemma-2-9b-it. Ask for a hint and the model answers correctly without ever writing the word. Ground truth is known — a laboratory liar, and the suspect in our live demo.
Logit lens
apply the final norm + unembedding to an intermediate layer, read the vocabulary distribution
Feed an intermediate layer straight into the model's own output head: the vocabulary words come out ranked. If the secret word sits in the top-5 at the middle layers while the output text never contains it, the model knows more than it says. This is the monitor in scene 10.

3 Tools · three probes, the same line ≈ 1 min
Paper: first appearance — parked at §2 Background, amber box on the "Probes." paragraph (page 2).

Our tools are three ways to draw the same line. All three are tiny — they generate nothing, they only judge.

LR is logistic regression — the classifier you all know, trained on labeled statements. (point at the first card)

MM is even simpler: take the center of the false cloud, the center of the true cloud, connect them. No training loop — that's a closed form. (point at the second card)

CCS is the interesting one. It gets no labels. It only demands one thing: a statement and its own negation must come out opposite. Remember that constraint — it becomes important in three slides. (point at the third card, pause a beat)

Three ways to draw the same line, all tiny — judgment only, no generation. LR: logistic regression on labeled statements. MM: connect the false-cloud center to the true-cloud center, closed form. CCS: no labels, only the constraint that a statement and its negation come out opposite. Remember that constraint.

5 The interesting failure · negation breaks the probe ≈ 1.5 min
Paper: scroll to Figure 5, amber box on the caption "The transfer failures live on negated statements" (page 5).

The failures live on negated statements. Same facts, same templates — we just put "It is not the case that" in front, and the label flips.

Look at the blue bar. LR trained on plain cities statements: 0.99 at home. Test it on the negated twins of the same sentences — 0.33. On negated translations — 0.28. Below chance. The probe is not confused; it is confidently wrong.

MM, the green bar, holds up near home — 0.77 — but falls to 0.16 on the transfer set.

Our reading: each template family writes truth in a slightly different direction, and negation rotates it. The model still knows the answer — the probe we trained just isn't looking at that direction anymore.

And the orange and purple bars say the fix is easy: add the negated pairs to training — or, next slide, drop the labels entirely. (advance)

7 The control · the answer is not in the words ≈ 1 min
Paper: scroll to Figure 7, amber box on the caption "Reading the text vs. reading the model" (page 6).

A fair suspicion: maybe the probe just reads the words, not the model. So we ran the control: take the raw text, never touch the model, train a TF-IDF classifier with the same 80/20 split.

Result: chance — 0.45 to 0.52 on ten of eleven datasets. A character-level variant reaches at most 0.55. The words alone don't tell you whether a statement is true.

There's exactly one leak, and it proves the rule. On larger-than it scores 0.94 — it picks up the digits. Train on larger-than, test on smaller-than, and it flips to 0.05. A surface digit trick, not the relation.

So truth is carried by the pairing of statement and world knowledge — and that lives in the model, not on the page.

The control for "is the probe just reading words?": raw text only, never the model, TF-IDF + LR, same 80/20 split. Result: chance on 10/11 (0.45–0.52); char-level ≤ 0.55. Q&A prep · the likely questions

The English answers are speakable as-is; the note under each is your own reminder.
Why does negation break the probe? Is the model confused by negation?
"The model isn't confused — the negated statements are answered correctly by the model itself. What breaks is the probe. Our reading is that each template family writes truth in a slightly different direction, and negation rotates it. Add the negated pairs to training and the probe recovers to 0.94 and above — so the knowledge was always there." Separate the two: the model is fine (it answers correctly), the probe is blind. Recovery to 0.94+ is the evidence.
CCS uses no labels — how can it match supervised probes?
"Because the supervision signal is hiding in the pairing. A statement and its negation have opposite truth values by construction, so requiring opposite outputs is a free label. CCS searches for the direction that satisfies that consistency, and it turns out to be the same direction the supervised probes find." The consistency constraint is itself a free label: a statement and its negation must differ in truth value. That one point is enough.
Your text baseline is at chance — but isn't the answer sometimes obvious from the words?
"Sometimes — and that's exactly the larger-than leak we show. The text classifier learns the digit trick, scores 0.94, and then flips to 0.05 on smaller-than. Surface cues don't transfer. On the other ten datasets the text is at chance, while the activation probe is near perfect. Same split, same data — the difference is whose representation you read." Concede the exception, then counter with 0.94→0.05. Stress "same split, same data — the difference is whose representation you read".
Why layer 14? Did you choose it, or the original authors?
"The original repo hard-codes layer 14 for LLaMA-2-13B. Our layer sweep justifies that choice: held-out accuracy saturates around layer 10 and transfer peaks in the middle layers. Fourteen sits right in the sweet spot, so we kept it for comparability." Layer 14 is the original repo's choice; our sweep supplies the justification — respect for the authors plus our own evidence.
Is the generalization matrix symmetric? Why not?
"Not exactly. Training on a narrow template family gives you a direction that's tuned to that family's geometry, so it may not cover a broader test set. The combinations — like larger-than plus smaller-than — train on more variety and transfer better. Coverage of the training data matters more than the algorithm." Asymmetry comes from training-set coverage. The combination datasets (larger+smaller) transferring best is the evidence.
Did you run the original code, or reimplement it?
"We ran the original Marks-and-Tegmark repo on a DGX Spark with LLaMA-2-13B, and reproduced their headline numbers. The midterm additions — the negation splits, the label-free comparison, and the text baseline — are our own code on top of the same cached activations and the same split protocol. The live Taboo catch is also our own script: the authors' public adapter, the authors' logit-lens protocol, real generation on this laptop." Two layers to name: original-repo reproduction + our own extensions. Same cached activations / same split is the rigor selling point; the demo adds a third, fully reproducible script (scripts/taboo_caught_demo.py).
What does this have to do with deceptive models and AI safety?
"Everything we've shown is about reading hidden semantics — what the model represents but doesn't say. A deceptive model is exactly the case where that gap matters, and the live catch shows it end to end: the text is clean, the activations are not. And notice which tool survived every test today: the label-free one. On the day you monitor for hidden intent, labels are precisely what you won't have." Bring the answer back to the two threads: the says-vs-knows gap + the label-free monitor. Do not promise experiments we have not run.

The page replays exactly that run — the answer text, the per-layer ranks, the verdicts; every number is computed from the run's hidden states, stored in results/taboo_caught.json. Nothing is hand-written, and the script is in our repo, so the whole catch reproduces with one command."
